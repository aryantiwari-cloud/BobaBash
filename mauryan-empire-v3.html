The Maurya Empire: 136 years that shaped a subcontinent

A kingdom on the Ganges became the first state to unite most of South Asia, and then, within two generations of its greatest ruler, it was gone. Scroll to watch the map grow and shrink.

c. 340 BCE

The richest kingdom on the Ganges

Magadha, ruled by the Nanda dynasty from Pataliputra, had money, river trade and a huge army. When Alexander’s soldiers refused to march past the Beas in 326 BCE, one reason given by Greek writers was fear of exactly this army.

The Nandas were rich, but according to later accounts they were not loved.

Pataliputra, near today’s Patna, stood where the Son meets the Ganges. Greek writers called it Palibothra.

c. 321 BCE

A young man and his adviser

Chandragupta Maurya overthrew the last Nanda king and took Pataliputra. Tradition credits a brahmin teacher, Chanakya, also called Kautilya, as the planner behind it.

The Arthashastra, a manual of statecraft linked to Kautilya, describes spies, taxes and armies in blunt detail. Scholars still argue about when it was actually written.

Sources disagree on Chandragupta’s origins. Buddhist tradition ties the name Maurya to a clan called the Moriyas, whose land was said to be full of peacocks. Greek and Indian texts both mention armed women guarding the king.

c. 305 BCE

Seleucus meets his match

Alexander’s old general Seleucus came east to recover his master’s conquests and found a state he could not beat. The deal he accepted handed Chandragupta lands in what is now Afghanistan and Baluchistan, and Seleucus got 500 war elephants in return.

His ambassador Megasthenes lived at Pataliputra and wrote a book about it. Only quotations from other authors survive.

Megasthenes, quoted by later writers, described Pataliputra as ringed by a wooden wall with hundreds of towers.

Ancient sources also mention a marriage alliance sealing the treaty, though they do not say exactly who married whom.

c. 297 BCE

Down into the Deccan

Jain tradition says Chandragupta gave up the throne and went south to fast in the manner of a monk. His son Bindusara then pushed the borders deep into the peninsula.

Remarkably little was written about him. What we have are fragments, a few Greek references and his son’s later fame.

Greek writers call him Amitrochates, usually explained as a version of a Sanskrit title meaning slayer of foes. He once asked Antiochus, Seleucus’s son, for sweet wine, dried figs and a philosopher. The wine and figs arrived; the reply was that philosophers were not for sale.

c. 300–260 BCE

How a huge state was run

No ruler could govern this much land from one city. Royal princes and trusted officials ran the regions, from Taxila in the northwest to Ujjain in the west. Tradition says Ashoka himself governed at Ujjain, and was sent to put down a revolt at Taxila, before he became king.

The other pillar was the army. Ancient writers credit Chandragupta with hundreds of thousands of soldiers and thousands of elephants. The figures may be inflated, but they show how outsiders saw him.

A great road, later called the Uttarapatha, ran from the northwest across the plains toward Pataliputra. The Arthashastra describes officials who checked prices, weights and measures along such routes. Trade ran largely on silver punch-marked coins.

c. 261 BCE

Kalinga, and a change of heart

Bindusara’s son Ashoka conquered Kalinga, roughly today’s Odisha, on the east coast. In his own words, carved on rock, about a hundred thousand people died and a hundred and fifty thousand were deported. He wrote that he felt remorse.

He turned to dhamma, a code of moral conduct, and had it inscribed on rocks and pillars from Afghanistan to Karnataka, in several languages. At its peak the empire covered roughly five million square kilometres, by common estimates.

Ashoka kept his army and still punished crime. Dhamma was a public ethic, not a retreat from ruling, and Rock Edict XIII even warns forest peoples not to cause trouble.

c. 250 BCE

Ashoka’s word on stone

His messages went up on cliffs and on polished sandstone pillars, some taller than a three-storey building and weighing tens of tonnes, hauled long distances from the quarries near Chunar. Most are in Prakrit written in Brahmi; in the northwest, Kharosthi, Greek and Aramaic were used instead.

The edicts speak of kindness to servants, tolerance between sects, medical care for people and animals, and wells and shade trees along roads. Envoys went abroad, and tradition says his son Mahinda carried Buddhism to Sri Lanka.

Ashoka called himself “Beloved of the Gods” and used the title Piyadasi. His own name appears in only a few inscriptions.

At Lumbini, the Buddha’s birthplace, a pillar records Ashoka’s visit and a tax cut: the village was to pay only an eighth of its produce.

c. 232 BCE

The empire outlives its emperor

Ashoka died after about thirty-seven years in power. The state he left behind was huge, expensive to run and held together by officials answerable to the king.

Little is known about his successors. Historians point to succession disputes, shrinking revenue, powerful provincial governors and pressure from Greek kingdoms in the northwest. They disagree about which mattered most.

Names such as Dasharatha and Samprati appear in later lists of Maurya kings, but Buddhist, Jain and Puranic king lists do not agree with one another.

c. 185 BCE

A parade, and an assassination

Later texts say the last Maurya king, Brihadratha, was killed while reviewing his troops by his own commander, Pushyamitra Shunga, who founded a new dynasty.

By then the Mauryas held little beyond the Magadha heartland, and the old empire had already broken into pieces.

The Shungas ruled much of central and eastern India for about a century. In the northwest, Bactrian Greeks moved into old Mauryan lands, and in the Deccan the Satavahanas became a major power.

After 185 BCE

Forgotten, then found

For centuries Ashoka was mostly a figure in Buddhist legend, and his inscriptions could not be read. In the 1830s James Prinsep deciphered the Brahmi script, and a king who called himself Piyadasi began to speak again.

It took until 1915, when an inscription at Maski named Ashoka outright, to be sure who he was. Today the Lion Capital from Sarnath is India’s national emblem, and the wheel at the centre of the national flag is the Ashoka Chakra, taken from the same capital.

One reading of the story is that a centralised state depends heavily on its ruler. Others stress money, regional governors and outside pressure, and the debate is still open.
